As an electronic music producer, I have encountered increasingly diverse ways of producing pop music over the years, as digital tools and software continue to evolve and generative AI becomes a new player in the music industry. As a researcher, I have become increasingly interested in how producers negotiate their creative agency amid these technologies, and what is recognized as their own creative contribution. I am particularly interested in how women producers navigate these changes, given how gendered environments and industry structures have historically shaped their creative agency (Wolfe 2019; Rogers 2010).

To explore these questions, I plan to combine digital methods with practice-based research and ethnography. As Smith and Dean (2009) suggest, creative practice can itself generate research knowledge, rather than simply illustrate theoretical arguments. In my own creative practice, I want to conduct comparative production experiments using four different workflows: preset-driven, sample-based, generative-AI-assisted, and acoustic recording. By producing different versions of the same musical material, I hope to examine how each workflow introduces different creative possibilities and constraints. Screen recordings, DAW project files, plugin settings, and production diaries would allow me to trace the decisions I make throughout the process. For instance, a preset might inspire a melodic idea I had not initially considered, raising the question of whether that idea originated from me, the software designer, or our interaction.

However, my own production experience represents only one perspective. Ethnographic research would allow me to investigate how other producers experience and negotiate these relationships. I plan to conduct interviews and studio observations with women music producers, primarily in China, while using the United States as a comparative context. I am interested in observing how producers interact with digital tools during their everyday creative processes, including moments of experimentation, hesitation, and collaboration. Interviews could reveal how they understand their own authorship and creative control, while observations might uncover decisions that producers do not consciously articulate. 
Combining these methods would allow me to examine musical decisions from multiple perspectives. Digital traces can document what happens within production software, while production diaries and ethnographic interviews can help explain why particular decisions were made. I am especially interested in moments when these forms of evidence do not align. For example, a producer might describe a sound as an entirely personal creative choice, while screen recordings reveal that it emerged from a plug-in preset. I want to understand how creative agency is distributed and negotiated through their interaction, including influences that producers themselves may not consciously recognize.

At the same time, digital methods cannot capture everything (Hampton 2017). Screen recordings document clicks and edits, but not necessarily intuition, emotion, social negotiation, or the gendered experiences that shape creative decisions. The technologies used to document creativity also determine which actions become visible as data. Ultimately, I want to use digital methods not just to document what decisions producers make, but also to understand why they make them, and how these decisions are shaped by technology, personal experiences, and the social environments in which they work.
