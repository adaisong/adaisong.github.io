Summary on the September 29 readings on critical making

All three readings raise a fundamental question about digital humanities: what makes the act of creating something a meaningful form of scholarship? Traditionally, humanities research has been associated with reading, interpretation, and writing. Digital humanities, however, has increasingly challenged this distinction by treating the production of digital objects as a way of generating knowledge. David Staley's “On the ‘Maker Turn’ in the Humanities,” Jeremy Boggs, Jennifer Reed, and J. K. Purdom Lindblad's “Making It Matter,” and Emily Johnson and Anastasia Salter's chapters “Map” and “Generation” offer different perspectives on this shift. But collectively they suggest that making can become a scholarly practice, but its value depends on how we interpret, question, and take responsibility for what we create.

In “On the ‘Maker Turn’ in the Humanities,” Staley (2017) challenges the assumption and tradition that scholarly arguments must primarily take the form of written texts. He considers how visual and digital forms can function as modes of interpretation rather than merely illustrating conclusions reached through conventional writing. The VideoWall where he juxtaposes text on screens instead of images, delivers an interesting message: the words on the screens are themselves a delivery of knowledge, and yet that’s not all of it. The way they’re presented through those screens, the arrangement of the screens, and the presentation of them as if they were visual products make the wall itself a way of thinking. It suggests that patterns themselves can communicate scholarly arguments. From this perspective, creating a digital object is not necessarily separate from thinking; the process of designing and constructing it may constitute an intellectual contribution.

However, does every act of making automatically become scholarship? Boggs, Reed, and Purdom Lindblad (2017) approach this issue from a more explicitly ethical perspective. In “Making It Matter,” they argue that the value of making depends not only on what is produced but also on the people, labor, and social relationships involved. Their discussion of the Scholars' Lab Makerspace introduces historical reconstruction as a form of experimental inquiry. They cite Devon Elliott and colleagues' use of 3D modeling and printing to investigate historical magic tricks. Rather than attempting to produce exact replicas, these researchers reconstruct technological artifacts to explore how they might have worked and to generate new historical questions. By physically experimenting with historical technologies, researchers may encounter practical difficulties that written descriptions do not reveal. Making becomes a way of investigating the limitations of archival evidence. The resulting object is not necessarily the final scholarly contribution; the questions discovered through its construction may be equally important.

Yet Boggs and colleagues also insist that experimentation cannot be separated from ethical responsibility. Their discussion of the Thistlewood Project offers a particularly powerful example. Thomas Thistlewood, an eighteenth-century Jamaican enslaver, documented 1,584 acts of rape against enslaved women in his diaries. Because the women themselves left no surviving written accounts, the project faces a fundamental problem: how can researchers represent victims whose experiences are documented almost entirely through the words of their oppressor?

The researchers respond through digital mapping. They create maps dedicated to individual women and use distinct visual markers to differentiate their experiences. They also include scans of Thistlewood's diaries as historical evidence. These decisions demonstrate that interface design is itself an interpretive and ethical practice. How information is organized and visualized influences whose experiences become visible and how viewers understand historical violence. Nevertheless, the project also reveals the limitations of critical making. Although the maps attempt to restore individuality to the victims, they remain dependent on the perpetrator's records. Digital representation cannot fully recover voices that were never preserved. This tension suggests that ethical design may challenge historical inequalities without necessarily overcoming them.
This tension raises a broader question: if digital technologies cannot fully escape the biases and limitations of their source materials, how can making with these tools still become a critical practice? Johnson and Salter's (2025) chapters “Map” and “Generation” extend this discussion to contemporary digital technologies. “Map” explores how mapping tools shape our understanding of space and representation, while “Generation” examines the possibilities and limitations of generative AI. Rather than treating these technologies as neutral instruments, the authors encourage experimentation as a way to question their underlying assumptions. Their approach suggests that critical making is not necessarily about overcoming technological limitations, but about making those limitations visible through the creative process.
