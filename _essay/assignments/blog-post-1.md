Data injustice is not new. When colonizers build museums in colonized lands, when they select what to put in the historic archives, they voices and choice, and perspectives created what we know as knowledge today. Artificial intelligence and machine learning don’t escape the same situation, as the fundamental question still exists: whose data are we actually working with? If certain communities, cultures, and histories have been excluded or misrepresented in the first place, can machine learning help address these inequalities, or will it simply reproduce them on a larger scale?

Patricia Murrieta-Flores's research uses handwriting recognition and language technologies to make centuries-old colonial documents more accessible for historical research (Murrieta-Flores 2026a). These technologies are not simply making archival research faster. They also create opportunities to recover histories and perspectives that have remained difficult to access. However, making colonial archives searchable does not necessarily mean that the information within them becomes neutral or representative. These documents were produced within particular structures of colonial power, and their biases remain even after digitization.
This connects to the questions raised in her workshop, Towards the Development of Decolonial Technologies. The workshop encouraged participants to reconsider how technologies are designed, including whose knowledge is represented, who controls the data, and who benefits from technological development (Murrieta-Flores 2026b). I agree that data injustice cannot be solved simply by collecting larger datasets. We also need to question how information is categorized, interpreted, and made available.

As a music producer and researcher working between China and the United States, I see similar questions emerging in music technologies. For example, machine learning models in the US are trained primarily on music accessible from western music platforms, while Chinese models are trained with Chinese music platforms. The two countries have developed largely different digital music ecosystems, with platforms such as Spotify and Apple Music in the United States and QQ Music and NetEase Cloud Music in China. These differences raise questions about what kinds of musical data are available to different AI developers and how they might shape the music generated by their models. On one hand, such differences and competition could be healthy, as no single platform or culture would define what AI-generated music should sound like. On the other hand, they could also reinforce existing cultural divisions and sonic stereotypes, limiting how each side understands the other's musical traditions. More importantly, whose musical knowledge is considered valuable enough to become part of the training data? Must the development of AI become another form of technological competition between nations? Could these systems reproduce the geopolitical boundaries that already shape access to knowledge?

I am also interested in how these inequalities affect women music producers. If music industry datasets primarily recognize commercially visible artists or established production credits, they may overlook creative contributions that are less formally documented. Machine learning could potentially help identify overlooked patterns and contributions across large collections of recordings, credits, and historical materials. However, this would require researchers to critically examine the datasets and categories they use, rather than assuming that computational analysis automatically produces objective results.

Ultimately, I think machine learning offers possibilities for confronting data injustice, but only when accompanied by critical attention to the histories and power structures behind the data. Making marginalized knowledge more visible is an important step, but visibility alone is not enough. We also need to ask who gets to represent that knowledge, how it is interpreted, and whether the communities involved have a say in how these technologies are developed.
