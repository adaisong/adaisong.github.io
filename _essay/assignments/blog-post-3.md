In my decade-long career as a music producer, I have witnessed how musicians' creative attention has shifted alongside technological developments. From spending hours perfecting chord progressions and fine-tuning the EQ of individual guitar tracks to clicking through endless variations of AI-generated sounds, the ways we engage with music production have changed significantly. Generative AI has introduced another major shift, allowing us to generate melodies, lyrics, arrangements, and even entire songs within seconds. While these tools offer new creative possibilities, I have become increasingly curious about what happens to the creative process when producing a finished piece becomes so easy. If AI can generate music almost instantly, does that necessarily make us more creative?

Johnson and Salter's Critical Making in the Age of AI offers an interesting perspective on this question. Their approach to critical making emphasizes the importance of experimentation, reflection, and learning through the process of making, rather than focusing exclusively on the finished product (Johnson and Salter 2025). This makes me think about how generative AI might be changing not only what we produce, but also where creative labor takes place. Instead of spending hours constructing a sound, producers might now spend that time generating, comparing, and selecting from hundreds of possibilities. The work does not necessarily disappear, but its nature changes.

I find this shift particularly interesting because selecting has always been part of music production. Even before generative AI, producers made countless decisions about which paddle to use, what effect chain to add onto a track, and what solo versions they want to choose. However, there seems to be a difference between manipulating a sound until it becomes something unexpected and repeatedly generating new material until something sounds right. In the latter case, how much of the creative process takes place through the producer's interaction with the system, and how much is already shaped by the system's underlying design? I do not think there is a simple answer, but this distinction makes me reconsider what we recognize as creative work.

Another question concerns whose creativity these systems represent. Generative AI models learn from existing musical materials, which raises questions about the cultural and artistic assumptions embedded in their outputs. As someone who works with Chinese musical elements in electronic pop production, I am particularly interested in how AI interprets musical traditions outside dominant Western pop conventions. Would it offer unfamiliar possibilities, or repeatedly generate recognizable stereotypes of what Chinese music should sound like? Experimenting with these limitations could itself become a form of critical making, allowing me to question the musical assumptions built into the technology.

Ultimately, I see critical making as an invitation to become more conscious of how we interact with generative AI. Rather than simply accepting a generated result, I want to experiment with what happens when I reject, manipulate, or deliberately challenge it. For me, the question is not whether AI-generated music counts as music, but how these technologies change the decisions, skills, and experiences involved in making it. Perhaps being a critical maker today means paying closer attention not only to what we create, but also to what we allow technology to create for us.
