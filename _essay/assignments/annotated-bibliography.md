## Tracing Musical Decisions in Digital Music Production
# Research Focus: Music Software, Platforms, Gender, and Creative Agency in Contemporary Music Production

I Software, Sampling, and Digital Sound
1. Arrieta, Alexandria. “Splice and the Platformization of Hip Hop Production: Navigating the Online Music Platform for Royalty-Free Samples.” Global Hip Hop Studies 2, no. 2 (2021): 219–236. https://doi.org/10.1386/ghhs_00045_1.
Arrieta examines how Splice, a platform for royalty-free samples, shapes hip-hop production by organizing how producers discover and access sounds. I selected this article because sample selection is a crucial yet frequently overlooked stage of contemporary production. Rather than analyzing the finished products and trace its samples, my research could extend this perspective by observing how producers navigate search terms, genre categories, and recommended sounds while making tracks. This books provides relevant context for my research. One limitation is its focus on hip-hop and Splice, which may not fully represent other genres or production environments.

2. D’Errico, Mike. Push: Software Design and the Cultural Politics of Music Production. Oxford University Press, 2022.
D’Errico investigates how music production technologies embody cultural assumptions about musical creativity. Instead of treating digital tools as neutral instruments, the book connects interface design with broader histories and politics of music-making. I chose this work because it directly addresses how technological design structures creative possibilities. In my dissertation, I plan to trace production decisions of women producers in particular across different digital audio workstations. I want to examine how presets, plug-ins, and interface features encourage certain actions while discouraging others. A potential limitation is that the study's emphasis on particular technologies may require adaptation when examining rapidly changing software ecosystems and generative AI tools.

3. Magnusson, Thor. Sonic Writing: Technologies of Material, Symbolic, and Signal Inscriptions. Bloomsbury Academic, 2019.
Magnusson explores how music technologies shape the ways we think about, create, and work with sound. He argues that instruments, notation, and digital tools are not simply neutral devices, but actively influence how musicians understand and make music. I selected this book because it provides a vocabulary for understanding plugins and software as active participants in musical thought rather than passive equipment. My research can use this framework to compare how producers conceptualize sound through presets, waveforms, and algorithmic processes. However, the book is more theoretical and does not offer many practical methods for studying how producers make decisions in their everyday work.

4. Brøvig-Hanssen, Ragnhild, and Anne Danielsen. Digital Signatures: The Impact of Digitization on Popular Music Sound. MIT Press, 2016.
Brøvig-Hanssen and Danielsen investigate how digital technologies have transformed the aesthetics of popular music, paying particular attention to audible traces of technological mediation. Through detailed musical and sonic analysis, they demonstrate how digital processes become recognizable components of musical expression. I chose this book because my project must examine not only what producers say about their decisions but also how those decisions materialize in sound. Its analytical approach could help me compare recordings created through different production workflows. One limitation is that sonic analysis alone cannot reliably reconstruct the intentions, negotiations, or social circumstances behind particular production choices.

II. Gender, Production, and Creative Authority
5. Wolfe, Paula. Women in the Studio: Creativity, Control and Gender in Popular Music Sound. Routledge, 2019.
Wolfe examines women's experiences in music production, emphasizing the relationship between technological access, creative autonomy, and gendered power structures. Her research challenges the assumption that access to recording equipment automatically guarantees artistic control. I selected this book because women producers make an important part of my proposed fieldwork in China and the US, and it resonates with my very own experience as a music creator in both industries. My project will extend the well-recorded studio environment into software-mediated and AI-assisted environments. A limitation is that the experiences documented in the book may not fully capture contemporary Chinese production cultures.

6. Rodgers, Tara. Pink Noises: Women on Electronic Music and Sound. Duke University Press, 2010.
Rodgers brings together interviews with women working in electronic music and sound, foregrounding their technical practices, artistic philosophies, and experiences within gendered musical cultures. The book offers an important methodological example of allowing practitioners to articulate their own relationships with technology. I chose it because my research combines interviews with selected women producer. Rodgers’s individualized approach can inform open-ended interview questions without presuming a unified category of women's musical expression. Its limitation is that interview narratives, while valuable, cannot independently reveal the moment-by-moment processes through which production decisions emerge.

III. Platforms, Algorithms, and Musical Value
7. Seaver, Nick. Computing Taste: Algorithms and the Makers of Music Recommendation. University of Chicago Press, 2022.
Seaver approaches music recommendation algorithms ethnographically, examining the people, institutions, and cultural assumptions involved in their development. He reveals algorithms’ dependence on human judgments and organizational priorities. I selected this book because my research similarly questions where agency resides when musical decisions involve computational systems. Its ethnographic approach can inform my investigation of how producers interpret platform recommendations and anticipate algorithmic visibility. A limitation is that Seaver primarily studies recommendation systems and their makers rather than musicians' creative workflows. My research would require methodological adaptation to production environments.

8. Bonini, Tiziano, and Alessandro Gandini. “‘First Week Is Editorial, Second Week Is Algorithmic’: Platform Gatekeepers and the Platformization of Music Curation.” Social Media + Society 5, no. 4 (2019). https://doi.org/10.1177/2056305119880006.
Bonini and Gandini investigate the relationship between human editorial decisions and algorithmic systems in music streaming curation. Their research complicates the distinction between human and automated gatekeeping by showing how both participate in determining musical visibility. I selected this article because producers increasingly work with an awareness of playlist placement, streaming metrics, and platform discoverability. My research can explore whether these expectations influence musical choices before a recording is released, including arrangement, duration, and genre labeling. A limitation is that the article focuses primarily on curation and distribution rather than directly documenting how platform expectations enter the studio.

9. Drott, Eric. Streaming Music, Streaming Capital. Duke University Press, 2024.
Drott situates music streaming within broader economic transformations, examining how platforms connect musical consumption, data extraction, and capital accumulation. His political-economic approach challenges understandings of streaming as merely a convenient distribution technology. I selected this book because musical decisions are not made independently of the infrastructures through which music circulates and acquires value. It can help my research connect individual production practices with the economic pressures generated by streaming platforms, particularly when comparing Chinese and American digital music industries. One limitation is that its macroeconomic perspective requires complementary ethnographic methods to explain how structural pressures become specific creative decisions.

10. Pelly, Liz. Mood Machine: The Rise of Spotify and the Costs of the Perfect Playlist. Atria/One Signal Publishers, 2025.
Pelly investigates Spotify's influence on musical labor, playlist culture, and listening experiences through a journalistic examination of the streaming industry. Her account raises questions about how platform business models shape musical production and the conditions under which artists work. I selected this book because it connects technological infrastructure with the lived consequences of platform-driven music economies. It complements Drott’s book with a micro-level perspective. My research could investigate whether producers internalize assumptions about playlist compatibility or algorithmic performance when composing and arranging tracks. A limitation is that its investigative journalistic methodology differs from academic ethnography, and its Spotify-centered perspective may not translate directly to Chinese platforms such as QQ Music or NetEase Cloud Music.

## Rationale for Source Selection and Discovery

I organized this bibliography into three areas based on my developing dissertation project, Who Decides What Music Sounds Like? Tracing Mediated Musical Decisions among Women Producers in China and the United States: gender and music production, music software and digital sound, and platforms and algorithms.

My interest in gender and music production came first. I had already encountered Paula Wolfe's Women in the Studio and Tara Rodgers's Pink Noises through my earlier research and writing on women in recording studios. For music technology, I found D'Errico's Push and Magnusson's Sonic Writing through Google searches using keywords such as "software design" and "music technologies." I also discovered Digital Signatures by following related book recommendations. Arrieta's article on Splice came from an AI-assisted search.

For the platform section, my advisor recommended Eric Drott's Streaming Music, Streaming Capital and Liz Pelly's Mood Machine. I encountered Nick Seaver's Computing Taste through a recommendation while reading another book about algorithms. Bonini and Gandini's article was suggested by ChatGPT.
